First: you are not bad at English, and you are not bad at learning

You did high school and college in Hong Kong and you scored Listening 9, Reading 9. Those are strong scores. You absorbed a lot of English — that part worked.

Your 9/9/7/6 split is the standard Hong Kong profile, and it's not a coincidence. HK English education is built around reading comprehension, grammar rules, and exam papers. Speaking gets almost no real practice, and when it does it's a scripted presentation, not a conversation. So you finished school with a big English database and almost no practice retrieving from it in real time. That's exactly what a 9/9/7/6 looks like.

"I suck at study" is probably the wrong conclusion. What you're bad at is the HK method — memorise, sit still, get tested. That method is genuinely poor at producing speakers, and disliking it says very little about you. Nothing in this plan is that method. There is no textbook, no rule memorisation, no exam papers to grind. It's closer to gym training than to school: short daily reps, out loud, and you track what you did rather than what you know.

Straight talk about the target

10 in all four bands is very rare, and S6 → S10 in 8 weeks is not a fair bet. What is very achievable: Writing 8–9, Speaking 8–9, which clears every immigration threshold that matters (CLB 9 = all 9s). Aim for 9s. If a 10 happens, good.

Don't book the test yet. Book at the end of Week 5, for a date in Week 8 or 9 — commit only once you can see the improvement yourself.

Why you're stuck at 6–7 — the Cantonese version

  • Cantonese doesn't mark grammar the way English does. No articles, no plural -s, no verb tense. You're not forgetting rules — your brain's default operating system doesn't have those slots. See the Cantonese Traps tab.
  • Half your "grammar errors" in speaking are actually pronunciation. Cantonese has no final consonant clusters, so books comes out as book and worked as work — even when you know the rule perfectly. The rater hears a grammar mistake. This is probably the single most fixable thing on your list.
  • Rhythm. Cantonese gives every syllable equal weight and a tone. English squashes unstressed syllables into "uh". That mismatch is why you sound choppy to them, and why fast native speech sounds like mush to you. Both problems, one cause.
  • You translate. Cantonese → English conversion costs 2–3 seconds a sentence. That gap is the hesitation the rater scores down.
  • You've never heard yourself. The fix is mechanical, not clever: record, transcribe, correct.

The three habits that do 80% of the work

  • 1. Shadowing (20 min/day). Play 2 minutes of native audio and speak at the same time, copying rhythm and intonation. This is the single fastest fix for sounding hesitant. Non-negotiable, every day.
  • 2. Record → transcribe → correct (30 min/day). Answer a task on your phone. Type out exactly what you said, mistakes included. Fix it. Re-record. Painful, and it works faster than anything else.
  • 3. Error log (10 min/day). Every mistake goes in the log tab. Anything that appears 3+ times is your real weakness — drill that, ignore the rest.

How to use these

These are scaffolds, not scripts. In Weeks 1–3 use them word-for-word — that's the point, it frees your brain to think about content instead of sentence shape. From Week 4, start varying the openers so you don't sound memorised. By Week 7 they should be invisible.

Universal rule: never stop talking. Silence is the harshest penalty in CELPIP speaking. If you blank, say "Let me put it another way —" or "The thing is," and keep going. A rambling answer scores far better than a clean answer with three long pauses.

Task 1 — Giving Advice

30s prep · 90s speaking · you're talking TO a person

Hey [name], I heard about ___, and I've got a few thoughts.

The first thing I'd suggest is ___, because ___.

Another option would be to ___. That way you could ___.

Honestly, if I were in your shoes, I'd go with ___.

Anyway, whatever you decide, let me know how it turns out.

90 seconds is long. You need three pieces of advice with a reason each, not two. Prep note: write only three words — one per piece of advice.

Task 2 — Personal Experience

30s prep · 60s · this is your storytelling weakness — drill it hardest

One time that really stands out for me was when ___.

This was about [X] years ago, back when I was ___.

What happened was, ___. And then ___.

I remember feeling really ___ at the time.

Looking back, it taught me that ___.

Past tense discipline. Pick past tense in sentence one and stay there for all 60 seconds. Mid-story tense slips are the #1 thing dragging a 7 down from a 9. Prepare 6 flexible real stories (a mistake, a success, a trip, a person who helped you, a hard decision, a first time) — almost any prompt bends to fit one of them.

Task 3 — Describing a Scene

30s prep · 60s · describe only what you SEE

This picture shows ___.

In the foreground, on the left, there's a man who's ___ing.

Next to him, two women are ___ing.

In the background, I can see ___.

Overall, it looks like a ___ kind of day / a pretty busy scene.

Two mechanical wins here: (1) every verb in present continuous — is walking, are sitting, is holding. (2) Use a fixed sweep so you never run out: foreground → left → right → background → overall. Also describe people's clothing and age — free words that fill time accurately.

Task 4 — Making Predictions

30s prep · 60s · same picture, now the future

In a few minutes, I think ___ is probably going to ___.

It looks like ___ will ___ once ___.

There's a good chance that ___.

I wouldn't be surprised if ___.

And by the end of the day, they'll most likely ___.

Range of future forms is what scores here — mix will, going to, might, probably, I expect. Using "will" five times caps you at 7.

Task 5 — Comparing and Persuading

60s prep · 60s · pick ONE option in the first 3 seconds

I've looked at both, and I really think we should go with ___.

Compared to the other one, this one is ___er / more ___.

The main reason is ___.

On top of that, ___.

I know you might be worried about ___, but honestly ___.

So what do you think — should we go for it?

Comparative grammar is being tested directly: cheaper than, more convenient than, not as big as, the best option. Force three comparatives in. And address the listener's objection — that one move separates 8 from 9.

Task 6 — Dealing with a Difficult Situation

60s prep · 60s · hardest task; choose your listener and commit

Hi ___, I'm really sorry, but I've got some bad news about ___.

The situation is that ___.

I know this isn't what you were hoping for, and I do apologise.

What I'd suggest is ___. Alternatively, we could ___.

Would either of those work for you?

The trap is switching listeners halfway ("I'll tell my boss... and I'd tell you..."). Decide in prep: one person, one whole minute. Politeness markers score points — I'm afraid, would it be possible, I completely understand, I do apologise.

Task 7 — Expressing Opinions

30s prep · 90s · PREP structure, two reasons

Personally, I strongly believe that ___.

There are two main reasons I feel this way.

First and most importantly, ___. For example, ___.

Second, ___. I've seen this myself when ___.

Now, some people would argue that ___, and I get that, but I'd say ___.

So overall, that's why I think ___.

90 seconds needs two reasons with a concrete example each. The counter-argument sentence is the highest-scoring line in the whole task — never skip it. Vague reasons ("it's better for people") score 6; specific examples score 9.

Task 8 — Describing an Unusual Situation

30s prep · 60s · describing a strange object to someone who can't see it

I want to tell you about something pretty strange I came across.

It's roughly the size of a ___, and it seems to be made of ___.

The top part looks kind of like a ___, and at the bottom there's ___.

It's a sort of dark green colour, with ___ on the side.

If I had to guess, I'd say it's used for ___ — but I'm honestly not sure.

Comparison is the scoring device: it's shaped like, it reminds me of, it's similar to, it's about as big as. Give size, material, colour, shape, parts, and a guess at purpose — six things, ten seconds each.

The recording loop (do this exactly)

  • 1. Voice memo on. Answer the prompt cold, on the clock. No stopping, no restarting.
  • 2. Type out exactly what you said — every "um", every wrong verb, every restart. This is the step people skip and it's the step that works.
  • 3. Mark your own errors first. Then paste to me and ask: "CELPIP band for this speaking transcript, plus my top 3 repeating errors."
  • 4. Add each error to the log tab.
  • 5. Re-record the same prompt, corrected. Notice how much easier the second one feels — that feeling is the skill forming.

Writing 7 → 9: what actually changes

A 7 covers the content but is repetitive and has recurring small errors. A 9 has varied sentence length, clean paragraphing, consistent tone, and errors so minor the reader doesn't notice. You do not need fancier vocabulary — you need fewer leaks and more sentence variety.

Word count: 150–200. Aim for 180. Under 150 is penalised; over 200 wastes proofreading time and adds errors.

Task 1 — Writing an Email

27 minutes · 150–200 words · read the tone cue carefully (friend = casual, manager/company = formal)

Time budget: 3 min plan → 18 min write → 6 min proofread. Protect the proofread. That's where your 7 becomes a 9.

Formal shape

Dear [Name / Sir or Madam],

I am writing to ___ regarding ___. — purpose in sentence one, always

[Paragraph 2 — bullet point 1, expanded into 3 sentences with specifics]

[Paragraph 3 — bullet points 2 and 3, with the reason behind your request]

I would greatly appreciate it if you could ___. Please let me know at your earliest convenience.

Sincerely,
Ivan

Casual shape

Hi [Name], · Hope you're doing well! I'm writing because ___. · Anyway, let me know what you think. Talk soon,

The three bullet points in the prompt are the grading rubric. Each one gets its own real paragraph with specific detail — a date, a number, a name. Missing or thin-covering one bullet caps you at 6 no matter how good the English is.

Task 2 — Responding to Survey Questions

26 minutes · 150–200 words · pick a side immediately, never balance

I would like to express my support for Option A. — first sentence, no hedging

There are two main reasons for my choice.

First and foremost, ___. In my neighbourhood, for instance, ___. This would mean that ___.

Secondly, ___. From my own experience, ___.

I do recognise that Option B would ___; however, I believe the benefits of Option A outweigh this concern because ___.

For these reasons, I strongly encourage the city to move forward with Option A.

Two reasons, each with a concrete invented example (a specific street, a number, a time of day — nobody is fact-checking you). The concession sentence + "however" is a reliable band-9 move. Sitting on the fence is the classic 6.

Your 6-minute proofread, in this order

  • Pass 1 — plurals & articles. Read every noun. Does it need a / the / -s? This alone will catch most of your errors.
  • Pass 2 — verbs. Every verb: right tense? Does it agree with its subject? He go → he goes.
  • Pass 3 — sentence length. Any sentence over 30 words? Split it. Three short sentences in a row? Join two. Variety is scored.
  • Pass 4 — bullet check. Point at each bullet in the prompt and find where you covered it. If you can't point to it, it isn't there.
Practise proofreading as its own skill. Twice a week, take an old piece of your writing and give yourself 6 minutes to find errors — count how many you catch. That number should climb every week.

"I barely understand what coworkers are saying"

With L9 you can understand English fine — what you're missing is fast casual speech, contractions, and idioms. Native speakers don't say "What did you do?", they say "Whadja do?". Test listening is recorded and clear; your coworkers are not.

The fix is connected speech, and shadowing unscripted conversation (not TED talks) is how you get it. Also: give yourself permission to say "Sorry, could you say that again?" Native speakers say it to each other constantly. Nodding when you didn't catch it is what actually damages your reputation at work.

Small talk — the 4 moves

Small talk isn't a conversation, it's a 30-second ritual with a fixed shape. Learn the shape and you never have to improvise.

1. Open — Morning! How's it going? Hey, how was your weekend? Busy week so far?

2. React (never just "oh ok") — Oh nice! That sounds fun. Oh no, really? Tell me about it.

3. Add one small thing about yourself — Yeah, same here — I ended up just ___. Funny you say that, I ___ over the weekend.

4. Exit cleanly — Anyway, I should get back to it. Catch you later! Alright, good chat — see you at standup.

Step 3 is the one you're probably skipping. If you only ask and react, the other person does all the work and it feels flat. One sentence about yourself is what turns it into a conversation.

Explaining a technical problem — the 5-beat structure

You said explaining reasons and telling the story is your weak spot. This is the fix. Five beats, always the same order, and you never lose the thread:

BeatSay
1. Headline firstSo the short version is: ___ is broken / we're behind on ___.
2. ImpactWhat that means for us is ___.
3. CauseThe reason it's happening is ___. Basically, ___.

Headline first is the habit to build. The instinct is to tell the story chronologically and arrive at the point last — that's what makes people look confused. Say the conclusion, then explain. Practise on every single problem you hit at work, out loud, alone, before you tell anyone.

Interview track — "talk like an expert"

Sounding expert has almost nothing to do with vocabulary. It's structure (you answer in a shape, not a ramble), specificity (numbers and names, not "some stuff"), and pace (slower than you think, with pauses in the right places).

Prepare 8 STAR stories, not 50 answers

Almost every behavioural question maps to one of these. Write all 8, drill them until they're 90 seconds each:

  • A hard technical problem you solved
  • A time you disagreed with a teammate or manager
  • A mistake you made and what you changed after
  • A project you led or drove without authority
  • A time you had to learn something fast
  • A time you handled conflicting priorities or a tight deadline
  • A time you helped someone else succeed
  • Something you built you're genuinely proud of

Your errors are systematic, not random

You said your grammar is "all wrong". It isn't — it's wrong in about nine predictable ways, and every one of them traces back to a place where Cantonese and English are built differently. Random errors are hard to fix. Systematic ones are easy, because you can hunt one pattern at a time.

Read this once. Then don't study it — just use it to recognise your own mistakes faster when you transcribe.

The nine Cantonese interference patterns

PatternWhat Cantonese doesWhat comes out in English
1. No articles Cantonese has no a or the. 我send email = "I send email", complete and correct. I sent email to manager → I sent an email to my manager
Your single most frequent error. Fix this one first.
2. Classifiers, not plurals 三本書 (saam1 bun2 syu1) = "three CL book". The number already shows plural, so the noun never changes. three ticket, many problem → three tickets, many problems
3. Aspect, not tense Verbs never change form. Time comes from adverbs (琴日 = yesterday) and particles: 咗 zo2 done, 緊 gan2 in-progress, 過 gwo3 ever-experienced. Yesterday I go there and I see him → Yesterday I went there and saw him
This is why your stories drift tense — you're marking time once at the start, Cantonese-style, then stopping.
4. No subject–verb agreement The verb is identical for I / you / he / they. There is nothing to agree with. He work here, it depend on → He works here, it depends on
5. Topic-first word order Cantonese fronts the topic: 呢套戲我睇咗 = "this movie, I watched already". This bug I already fixed it → I've already fixed this bug
Sounds "off" to Canadians without them being able to say why.
6. Adjectives are verbs 佢好高 = "he very tall". No is needed — 高 already functions as the verb. The server very slow → The server is very slow
I very like it → I really like it
7. Serial clauses, no connectors Cantonese strings clauses together freely without and / so / because. Build failed, I check log, it was memory → The build failed, so I checked the logs. It turned out to be memory.
This is the run-on habit that makes your explanations hard to follow.
8. Question particles & A-not-A 你去唔去? = "you go-not-go?" Questions are made with particles or repetition, never by moving words. I don't know what is the problem → I don't know what the problem is
You finish already or not? → Have you finished yet?
9. No present perfect equivalent Duration is expressed flatly: 我做咗三年 = "I do already three year". I work here for 3 years → I've been working here for 3 years
High-value fix — this exact sentence comes up in every interview.
Do not try to fix all nine. Take #1 (articles) in Week 1–2, #3 (tense) in Week 3–4, and let the error log tell you which of the rest are actually yours. Some won't be.

The HK formality trap — this one costs you CELPIP marks

HK business English is taught in a formal, officialese register: Please be informed that... Kindly note... Herewith attached... We would like to express our sincere apologies for the inconvenience caused.

Two problems. First, CELPIP Writing Task 1 often asks for a casual email to a friend or neighbour — writing it in stiff business register is a tone mismatch and gets marked down even though every sentence is grammatical. Second, in a Canadian tech workplace this register reads as cold or sarcastic. Nobody writes "kindly note" in Slack.

Cultural gaps that read as "poor English" but aren't

  • "We" instead of "I" in interviews. Chinese-influenced modesty makes you credit the team. Canadian interviewers read that as "you didn't actually do it." In a STAR answer, the Action section is always "I" — I decided, I built, I pushed back. You can credit the team in one sentence at the end. This single change moves interview outcomes more than any grammar fix.
  • Waiting to be asked. HK meeting culture waits for the senior person to invite you. Canadian meetings assume silence means you have nothing. Prepare one point before every meeting and say it in the first ten minutes — early, so it doesn't get harder.
  • Disagreeing feels rude. It isn't, and never disagreeing makes you look disengaged rather than polite. I see it a bit differently and my worry would be ___ are completely safe with a manager here.
  • "I don't know" feels like losing face. In Canadian tech it's the opposite — I'm not sure, let me check and get back to you reads as senior and honest. Nodding when you didn't catch something is the thing that actually damages your reputation.
  • Small talk isn't small. It feels pointless and intrusive coming from HK office norms. Here it's the entire mechanism by which people decide they trust you. The weekend question is not nosy — it's the handshake.
  • Self-deprecation lands differently. "My English is very poor, sorry" is normal humility in HK and reads in Canada as an actual warning label. Drop it. If you need to flag it: Bear with me, I'm still working on my English — said once, lightly, then never again.

The insight that matters most on this whole page

Some of your "grammar errors" in speaking are pronunciation errors.

Cantonese has no final consonant clusters, and its final stops (-p, -t, -k) are unreleased — you close your mouth but never let the air out. So when you say three books, the -s genuinely doesn't come out. When you say I worked late, the -ed vanishes. You know the rule. You applied the rule. The rater still hears "three book" and marks a grammar error.

This means a chunk of your Speaking 6 may be fixable without learning any grammar at all — just by physically finishing your words. It's also the fastest-moving item here: two weeks of daily drilling produces audible change.

Test it yourself today. Record: "She asked me for the sixth time last month, and I texted her the facts." Play it back. Count how many endings actually survived.

Your five sound targets, in priority order

TargetWhat's happeningThe drill
1. Final -s and -ed
highest value		 Dropped entirely. Kills plurals, third-person verbs, possessives, and all past tense at once. Say these slowly, exaggerating the ending until it feels silly: bookssss, worksss, needsss, walkeddd, fixeddd, askeddd. Then in sentences: He works. She asked. Three books. It depends. 3 minutes daily.
2. Consonant clusters Cantonese has none, so they collapse: asked → ask, texts → tex, world → wor, months → mon. Hardest first: asked, texts, months, sixths, strengths, twelfths, clothes, desks, risks. Slow, every consonant audible, then speed up. Don't add a vowel ("ask-ed") — just release the cluster.
3. Stress-timing (rhythm)
biggest effect on sounding natural		 Cantonese gives every syllable equal length and a tone. English squashes unstressed syllables into a weak "uh" (schwa) and stretches the stressed ones. Equal-weight English sounds robotic and choppy. Tap the table on the stressed syllable only: I WENT to the STORE to buy some MILK — three taps, and everything between them gets crushed. "to the" becomes "tuhthuh". Do this with every shadowing clip.
4. th / v / r th → f or d (three→free, this→dis). v → w or f (very→wery, have→haf). r → w or l. th: tongue tip lightly between the teeth, blow. think, three, both, month / this, that, they, other.
v: top teeth on bottom lip, voiced — feel the buzz. very, video, have, five, believe. Contrast pairs: very/berry, vine/wine.
5. Intonation range Cantonese uses pitch to distinguish words (6 tones), so using pitch for emotion and emphasis feels unnatural. Result: flat delivery, which CELPIP raters score as limited range and coworkers read as disinterested. Take one sentence — "I didn't say he stole the money." Say it seven times, stressing a different word each time. Notice the meaning changes each time. Then deliberately over-do the pitch on your CELPIP recordings; it'll feel theatrical and sound normal.

Don't study grammar. Study your grammar.

Working through a grammar book is a bad use of your 8 weeks. You have maybe 8–10 recurring errors that account for nearly all your lost points. Find them with the error log, then drill only those.

How to actually fix an error (not just notice it)

  • 1. Catch it in the log with the wrong version and the right version side by side.
  • 2. Write 10 correct sentences using that pattern, about your real life. Not textbook sentences — your job, your weekend.
  • 3. Say all 10 out loud. Grammar you've only written doesn't survive into speech.
  • 4. Next day, pick 3 and say them again from memory. Then again 3 days later.
  • 5. Spot-check your next transcript specifically for that one error. When you go two weeks clean, retire it from the log.
One error at a time. Trying to fix five at once means fixing none — while you're watching articles you'll drop the plurals. Pick your worst one each week, hunt only that one.

The rest of Grammar Review Day (after the test)

  • 1. Read the whole error log out loud. Both columns — the wrong version then the right one. Hearing your own mistake immediately followed by the fix is what makes it stick.
  • 2. Self-test the log. Cover the "correct" column. Fix each of your own logged errors from memory. Anything you miss stays on the list another week.
  • 3. Pattern sweep. Take one transcript from this week and hunt the nine Cantonese patterns one at a time — read it once looking only for articles, again looking only for plurals, and so on. Nine passes sounds slow; it catches roughly three times what one general read catches.
  • 4. Rewrite ten. Pick 10 logged errors, write the correct version from memory, say each aloud twice.
  • 5. Retire and re-target. Anything clean for two weeks comes off the list. Whatever's left appearing 3+ times is next week's single focus.
Why weekly and not one big day: a single grammar review day gets forgotten within about ten days — that's just how memory works, and it's exactly what happened with every grammar sheet you were given in school. Six shorter passes spaced a week apart beat one long session by a wide margin, and it's the spacing doing the work, not the effort.
